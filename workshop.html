Trust Me, I’m an Ecologist?

Learning to better judge evidence

Matthew Grainger

Norwegian Institute for Nature Research (NINA)

Welcome participants. Do not start with definitions. Tell them the afternoon is about making defensible judgements, not catching researchers out.

Timing: 2 minutes.

Does conservation work?

“In two-thirds of cases, conservation either improved the state of biodiversity or at least slowed declines.”

intervention trials

intervention types

Introduce only the information visible here. The paper describes a global meta-analysis comparing temporal biodiversity outcomes under conservation action with counterfactual outcomes. Do not yet discuss selection, study validity or the meaning of a trial.

[Sources] - Langhammer PF et al. 2024. The positive impact of conservation action. Science 384:453–458. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adj6598 [/Sources]

Timing: 3 minutes.

Ask for a show of hands or use coloured voting cards. Record the rough distribution, but do not discuss the study yet. Say: “Keep hold of that judgement—we will return to it.”

Timing: 5 minutes.

What else would you need to know?

Before accepting—or rejecting—the conclusion, what information would most change your confidence?

Write down one question, then compare it with the person beside you.

Collect questions without answering them. Likely requests include the search and eligibility criteria, representativeness, publication processes, credibility of counterfactuals, primary-study risk of bias, independence of trials, outcome harmonisation and heterogeneity. Group responses under study validity, synthesis validity and generalisability.

[Sources] - Langhammer et al. 2024. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adj6598 [/Sources]

Timing: 5 minutes.

What initially influenced you?

The journal?
The word “global”?
The number of studies?
The meta-analysis?
A conclusion you wanted to be true?

Take three or four responses. Surface the difference between reasons for trusting a source and evidence that directly supports the inference. Do not imply that prestige or scale are irrelevant; they are simply not substitutes for appraisal.

Timing: 3 minutes.

Today’s question is deliberately simple

How much confidence should I place in this claim—and why?

By the end, you should be able to:

  • distinguish an interesting result from credible evidence;
  • identify the largest threats to an ecological inference;
  • make a transparent, calibrated judgement;
  • say what additional evidence would most change your mind.

Emphasise that this is not a comprehensive risk-of-bias course. It is a practical reasoning toolkit.

Timing: 4 minutes.

Evidence does not speak for itself

What we observe

  • measurements
  • estimated effects
  • patterns
  • uncertainty
→

What we conclude

  • what caused the pattern
  • where it applies
  • whether it matters
  • what should be done
Critical appraisal examines the bridge between them.

Use an example from the room if possible. An effect estimate is not yet a causal or management conclusion.

Timing: 5 minutes.

Four different questions are often collapsed into one

  1. Is the result precisely estimated?
  2. Is the comparison internally credible?
  3. Does it apply in this ecological context?
  4. Is it useful for this decision?

A study can perform well on one question and poorly on another.

Invite quick examples: a precise but confounded observational association; a rigorous mesocosm experiment with limited field transferability.

Timing: 5 minutes.

Ecology has a risk-of-bias problem

12%

correctly interpreted risk of bias

4%

had actually used a risk-of-bias tool

5

of 209 relevant journal policies explicitly referred to methodological standards including RoB or critical appraisal

Risk of bias is central to inference—but remains peripheral in ecological practice.

These results come from 232 ecologists and evolutionary biologists with evidence-synthesis experience, alongside an assessment of 275 journal policies. Explain that many respondents confused risk of bias with publication bias.

[Sources] - Culina A, Grainger MJ, O’Dea RE, Pescott OL, Sánchez-Tójar A. 2026. Elevating the importance of Risk of Bias assessment for ecology and evolutionary biology. Methods in Ecology and Evolution. https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.70356 [/Sources]

Timing: 6 minutes.

Risk of bias is not a synonym for “study quality”

Risk of bias

Could systematic error make the expected estimate differ from the study’s target effect?

Other important attributes

Precision, reporting, reproducibility, relevance and external validity.

All matter—but combining them in a single score hides how each problem changes the inference.

Use the contrast between internal and external validity. A small sample may be imprecise without being systematically biased; poor reporting can make bias impossible to judge without proving that bias occurred.

[Sources] - Culina et al. 2026. https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.70356 [/Sources]

Timing: 5 minutes.

Five questions reveal most of the structure

Ask
Randomisation Were experimental units assigned randomly?
Selection Could inclusion in the sample depend on relevant characteristics?
Confounding Could a common cause explain both exposure and outcome?
Measurement Were outcomes obtained comparably across groups?
Blinding Could knowledge of allocation influence observation or analysis?

These are the five introductory questions synthesised in Culina et al. They help readers begin an appraisal; they are not a universal replacement for design-specific tools. Tell participants explicitly not to add up ticks.

[Sources] - Culina et al. 2026. Section 4.2. https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.70356 [/Sources]

Timing: 7 minutes.

A signalling question informs—but does not replace—judgement

Question → Evidence → Bias mechanism → Judgement

Allocation was not random. Baseline habitat quality affected both treatment placement and breeding success. The comparison is therefore vulnerable to confounding that may exaggerate the benefit.

Distinguish a factual response from the judgement it informs. The reasoned statement is the useful output—not a coloured cell on its own.

[Sources] - Boyd et al. 2022. https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.13857 - Culina et al. 2026. https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.70356 [/Sources]

Timing: 4 minutes.

Appraisal is a judgement, not a score

Higher confidence Some concerns Low confidence Cannot determine

For every judgement, record:

  1. the evidence that supports it;
  2. the limitation that matters most;
  3. the likely direction of distortion—if knowable.

Stress that “not reported” is different from “not done”, but both limit what a reader can conclude. Avoid false precision from summed quality scores.

Timing: 5 minutes.

Exercise 1 · Which study do you trust?

In groups, rank four studies addressing the same ecological question.

For each study:

  1. What can it legitimately tell us?
  2. What is its most serious limitation?
  3. Would a larger sample solve that problem?
  4. What information is missing?

You have 12 minutes. Be ready to defend—not merely report—your ranking.

Distribute Exercise 1 cards. Use groups of about five. Ask each group to choose a reporter.

Timing: 12 minutes activity + 8 minutes debrief.

The question

Does restricting visitor access improve breeding success in ground-nesting birds?

All four studies report higher breeding success where disturbance was lower.

This remains on screen while groups work if the printed cards contain the study details.

A useful ranking needs a reason

Do not ask only:

Which study sits highest in a hierarchy?

Also ask:

  • Which bias is plausible here?
  • Which estimate could it distort?
  • In what direction?
  • Would it change the decision?

During debrief, expect reasonable disagreement. Reward explicit reasoning. A large observational study may be more applicable but less causally secure than a smaller controlled intervention.

More observations do not repair the wrong comparison

Precision

More independent information can reduce sampling uncertainty.

Bias

More measurements can estimate a biased contrast with exquisite precision.

Large n answers “how precisely?”—not automatically “how credibly?”

Transition from Exercise 1. Use an example in which accessible sites systematically differ from restricted sites.

Timing: 4 minutes.

Count the units that could have differed

One grazed field and one ungrazed field.
Twenty quadrats are sampled in each field.

How many independent replicates of grazing are there?

One field per condition—not twenty.

Ask first. Then reveal. Explain treatment replication versus measurement replication. Avoid saying subsamples are useless; they characterise within-unit variation but do not create treatment-level replication.

Timing: 5 minutes.

The design determines what is exchangeable

Units assigned or exposed

Fields, lakes, populations, years, transects—or individuals?

Units measured

Quadrats, samples, observations, pixels—or repeated visits?

The analysis must respect how variation entered the study.

This is a conceptual slide, not a technical mixed-model lesson. Mention clustering and repeated measurements only briefly.

Timing: 4 minutes.

Exercise 2 · A rainforest food web is collapsing

Arthropod abundance declined dramatically between historical and contemporary samples in Puerto Rico’s Luquillo rainforest; warming was proposed as the major driver, with consequences for insectivores.

Using the paper extract:

  1. Separate the trend claim from the causal claim.
  2. Define the population in space, time and taxa.
  3. Identify the most serious potential bias.
  4. State what the data can—and cannot—support.

You have 12 minutes.

Use the Lister and Garcia exercise sheet. Do not distribute the Willig et al. response yet. Ask groups to focus on one bias mechanism rather than producing a long list of limitations.

[Sources] - Lister BC, Garcia A. 2018. Climate-driven declines in arthropod abundance restructure a rainforest food web. PNAS 115:E10397–E10406. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1722477115 [/Sources]

Timing: 12 minutes activity + 10 minutes reveal/debrief.

ROBITT begins before examining the dataset

What exactly is the inferential goal—and what is the corresponding statistical target population?

For temporal trends, define the relevant axes:

  • space;
  • time;
  • taxonomy;
  • environment, where relevant.

ROBITT contains 17 signalling questions. Its distinctive first move is to require a defined target population before asking whether the sample represents it.

[Sources] - Boyd RJ et al. 2022. ROBITT: A tool for assessing the risk-of-bias in studies of temporal trends in ecology. Methods in Ecology and Evolution 13:1497–1507. https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.13857 [/Sources]

More biodiversity records do not guarantee better inference

Opportunistic records may change through time in:

  • where observers visit;
  • which taxa attract attention;
  • survey effort and technology;
  • detection and reporting;
  • the combination of datasets and protocols.
Time can become confounded with the way observations entered the dataset.

Explain that a huge non-probability sample can still be systematically unrepresentative. This is particularly important for aggregated occurrence data.

[Sources] - Boyd et al. 2022. https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.13857 [/Sources]

Reveal · Another team reached a different judgement

Willig et al. questioned:

  • comparability and construction of the temperature record;
  • adjustment for variation in sampling effort;
  • omission of some available pre-hurricane data;
  • hurricanes and secondary succession as competing explanations;
  • extrapolation from selected series to food-web collapse.

Which concerns match your appraisal—and which actually threaten the stated inference?

Distribute the short Willig et al. response. Do not present it as the answer key. It is another evidence-based argument that must itself be appraised.

[Sources] - Willig MR et al. 2019. Populations are not declining and food webs are not collapsing at the Luquillo Experimental Forest. PNAS 116:12143–12144. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1820456116 [/Sources]

Timing: 5 minutes.

The response is not the final word

Lister and Garcia replied that their conclusions were supported by consistent patterns across datasets and challenged aspects of the reanalysis.

Discuss:

  • What evidence would discriminate between the explanations?
  • Does disagreement concern the trend, its cause, its generality—or all three?
  • What conclusion remains defensible despite the dispute?

The learning objective is calibrated judgement, not declaring a winner.

[Sources] - Lister BC, Garcia A. 2019. Reply to Willig et al.: Long-term population trends in the Luquillo rainforest. PNAS 116:12145–12146. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1904582116 [/Sources]

Timing: 6 minutes.

Association can still be valuable

Observational evidence can:

  • reveal patterns at realistic scales;
  • include exposures that cannot be assigned;
  • test predictions across contexts;
  • narrow competing explanations;
  • complement experiments, models and natural experiments.

The question is not “experiment or useless?” It is “which explanations remain plausible?”

Prevent the session becoming a simplistic hierarchy of evidence. Ecological inference often depends on triangulation.

Timing: 4 minutes.

Break

Ten minutes

When we return: what happens when every study is imperfect—and they disagree?

Aim to restart at 14:35. Put the next exercise cards on tables during the break.

A body of evidence has its own failure modes

Even if each result is calculated correctly:

  • studies may ask different questions;
  • outcomes may be measured differently;
  • several papers may reuse the same data;
  • missing studies and outcomes may favour a story;
  • synthesis may ignore precision or study validity.

Transition from study-level appraisal to synthesis-level appraisal.

Timing: 5 minutes.

Eight studies, one tempting conclusion

+ + + + + 0 0 −

“Five of eight studies found a positive effect.”

What have we not been told?

Gather answers: magnitude, uncertainty, study size, design, independence, outcome definitions, selective reporting, context.

Timing: 4 minutes.

Significance is not a unit of evidence

Two studies can estimate the same effect but produce different p-values.

Two studies can produce the same p-value but estimate very different effects.

Counting “positive” and “negative” studies discards magnitude, precision and comparability.

Explain this without equations unless the audience asks. Distinguish effect-direction vote counting from a structured synthesis of effect estimates.

Timing: 5 minutes.

Exercise 3 · Can this review estimate global insect decline?

Sánchez-Bayo and Wyckhuys synthesised reports of insect decline and proposed widely cited global conclusions.

Produce:

  1. the question its search could answer;
  2. ways search and inclusion could select on the result;
  3. a comparison between the evidence and the implied global target population;
  4. a conclusion that matches the evidence.

You have 16 minutes. Separate concern for insects from confidence in this particular estimate.

Distribute the review exercise sheet. Then reveal the summaries of published responses. The point is selection and generalisation, not recalculating the review.

[Sources] - Sánchez-Bayo F, Wyckhuys KAG. 2019. Worldwide decline of the entomofauna: A review of its drivers. Biological Conservation 232:8–27. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2019.01.020 - Simmons BI et al. 2019. Worldwide insect declines: An important message, but interpret with caution. Ecology and Evolution 9:3678–3680. https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.5153 - Montgomery GA et al. 2020. Is the insect apocalypse upon us? How to find out. Biological Conservation 241:108327. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2019.108327 [/Sources]

Timing: 16 minutes activity + 10 minutes debrief.

Selection can happen before the analysis begins

If a review searches principally for documented declines, it can describe reported declines—but cannot estimate the prevalence of decline without an appropriate denominator.

Defensible

What patterns and drivers are reported among selected decline studies?

Not identified

What proportion of all insect populations worldwide is declining?

This is selection on the outcome. Ask whether a transparent search can nevertheless be systematically unsuitable for the broader inference.

[Sources] - Sánchez-Bayo & Wyckhuys 2019. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2019.01.020 - Simmons et al. 2019. https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.5153 [/Sources]

Timing: 5 minutes.

Optional advanced case · Historical bumblebee records

Soroye et al. used historical occurrence data to estimate bumblebee change across Europe and North America. Guzman et al. subsequently argued that the framework could yield biased estimates and proposed an alternative.

Apply ROBITT:

  • What is the target population?
  • Which sampling processes changed through time?
  • What does a non-detection mean in historical records?
  • Which assumptions connect occurrence records to decline?

Use this for faster or more quantitative groups, or retain it as a backup. The original authors also published a reply; this is another scientific disagreement, not a solved classroom puzzle.

[Sources] - Soroye P, Newbold T, Kerr J. 2020. Climate change contributes to widespread declines among bumble bees across continents. Science 367:685–688. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aax8591 - Guzman LM et al. 2021. Using historical data to estimate bumble bee occurrence. Biological Conservation 257:109180. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2021.109180 [/Sources]

Heterogeneity is information—not merely noise

When effects differ, ask whether variation follows:

  • species or functional group;
  • intervention intensity;
  • baseline condition;
  • climate or landscape context;
  • follow-up duration;
  • study design or risk of bias.
“Does it work?” may be the wrong question. Try “when, where and for whom?”

Connect responses from Exercise 3 to ecological context dependence. Avoid suggesting that every post-hoc subgroup explanation is credible.

Timing: 5 minutes.

Do not explain every pattern after seeing it

More credible

  • specified before analysis
  • supported by mechanism
  • replicated across studies
  • distinguishes competing predictions

Less credible

  • selected because it “worked”
  • many moderators tested
  • few studies per comparison
  • story changes with the model

Introduce researcher degrees of freedom and over-interpretation of subgroup/meta-regression patterns.

Timing: 5 minutes.

Absence of evidence has several meanings

No studies

We do not know because the question has not been studied.

Imprecise evidence

Existing studies cannot distinguish important benefit from harm.

Evidence of little effect

Precise estimates exclude effects large enough to matter.

Ask participants which of these is most often described as “no effect”. Reinforce that null statistical significance does not identify the third case.

Timing: 5 minutes.

Decisions require more than an effect estimate

Credibility + Applicability + Benefits and harms + Costs + Values

Low confidence does not automatically mean “do nothing”. It means uncertainty must be carried into the decision.

Distinguish a scientific conclusion from a management recommendation. Mention reversibility, monitoring, opportunity costs and precaution as examples of decision considerations.

Timing: 5 minutes.

Exercise 4 · The evidence tribunal

Claimant

Make the strongest defensible case that the intervention works.

Sceptic

Identify the weakness most likely to change the conclusion.

Decision-maker

Decide what action is justified—and under what conditions.

Reporter: capture the judgement, reasoning and unresolved uncertainty.

Use the evidence from Exercise 3. Assign roles within each group. Give 8 minutes for deliberation and 7 minutes for reports from selected groups.

Timing: 15 minutes.

A defensible conclusion has four parts

The evidence suggests [what], in [which contexts].
Our confidence is [level] because [reason].
The conclusion is most sensitive to [limitation or missing evidence].
For this decision, we would [act / monitor / test / wait] because [trade-off].

Give groups this structure before their reports. Point out that cautious language alone is not transparent unless the reasons are specified.

Timing: 4 minutes.

Return to Langhammer et al.

How were the 665 trials classified?

Outcome relative to the counterfactual Trials
Biodiversity improved under conservation 45.4%
Biodiversity declined more slowly 20.6%
Biodiversity declined more under intervention 20.6%
Biodiversity improved more under the counterfactual 11.6%
No difference 1.8%

Is “two-thirds of trials” an effect estimate, a vote count—or a descriptive classification?

The first two categories sum to 66%. Ask what information the classification discards: magnitude, precision, study validity, context and dependence. Do not imply that the paper relied only on this classification; it also reported a quantitative meta-analysis.

[Sources] - Langhammer et al. 2024. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adj6598 [/Sources]

Timing: 6 minutes.

Which conservation evidence entered the synthesis?

  • A Rapid Evidence Assessment retrieved 1,445 English-language publications.
  • Only 186 supplied temporal intervention–counterfactual comparisons that could be converted to the chosen effect size.
  • 95 of 186 studies came from Western Europe, North America, Australia and New Zealand.
  • Protected areas and invasive/problematic species control contributed 63% of the included studies.
  • Multiple metrics or species from one study could contribute separate trials, modelled as nested within studies.
  • [Also the reported Hedges g is implausible]

Ask which statements bear on internal validity, external validity, precision or synthesis-level selection. The authors conducted sensitivity analyses for nesting and a supplemental search. Avoid calling the 665 trials simple pseudoreplication.

[Sources] - Langhammer et al. 2024. Main text and supplementary methods. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adj6598 [/Sources]

Timing: 6 minutes.

The evidence is useful—but the target matters

More defensible

Among eligible, evaluated interventions, conservation often produced better biodiversity trajectories than the selected counterfactuals.

Not directly estimated

Conservation actions generally have a two-thirds probability of succeeding across actions, places, organisms and circumstances.

The synthesis estimates outcomes in the available evaluated comparisons—not every possible conservation action.

This is the key generalisability and estimand point. The paper provides substantive evidence of beneficial action; the critique concerns the breadth and interpretation of the headline claim, not whether conservation can work.

[Sources] - Langhammer et al. 2024. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adj6598 [/Sources]

Timing: 5 minutes.

Vote again

How confident are you that the study shows “conservation works”?

Very confident Fairly confident Uncertain Not confident

Changing your mind when the evidential basis changes is good scientific judgement.

Repeat the vote. Ask people who moved in either direction what information changed their judgement. Distinguish confidence that conservation can be beneficial, confidence in the average result among included comparisons, and the probability that a new action will succeed.

[Sources] - Langhammer et al. 2024. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adj6598 [/Sources]

Timing: 5 minutes.

Before trusting a claim, pause

Compared with what?

What else could produce this pattern?

What is genuinely replicated?

What is missing—and could it change the story?

These are the four questions to remember if CLAIM itself is forgotten.

Timing: 3 minutes.

One question to take with you

The next time I encounter a confident ecological claim, I will ask…

Write your question down. Then share it with the person beside you.

Allow one minute to write and one minute to share. Invite two examples. Close by thanking participants for making their reasoning visible.
